Sharing a pool with other people

To let more than one person log the same pool, have everyone connect to the same backup destination below — the same Dropbox/Google Drive/OneDrive account, or the same synced local folder. Whoever backs up or restores gets everyone's entries merged together automatically; no one's log overwrites anyone else's. It's not instant like a live feed — each person needs to back up after logging, and restore (or just back up again) to pull in what others added — but it's enough for a household or small team sharing a pool. Turning on "Auto-save after every log entry" on whichever method you use makes it feel close to automatic.

State & county pool testing rules — what's out there

This page is general information, not legal advice. It only matters for public/commercial pools (hotels, HOA/community pools, gyms, rentals) regulated by a state or county health department — a private residential pool generally isn't subject to any of this. Rules vary by state, county, and even city, and change over time. If you operate a regulated facility, always check with your local health department / permit for the rules that actually apply to you. Liquid Ledger doesn't enforce or certify compliance with any of these — it just lets you track whatever parameters and ranges you choose.

What's common across most jurisdictions

  • Free chlorine (or bromine) residual and pH are tested and logged everywhere, with no exceptions found.
  • Many jurisdictions also require logging total chlorine, combined chlorine, total alkalinity, cyanuric acid (CYA), calcium hardness, water temperature (especially spas), and water clarity (main drain visible).
  • Typical acceptable ranges: pH 7.2–7.8; free chlorine ≥1 ppm in pools (≥2–3 ppm with CYA present or in spas), generally capped at 10 ppm; combined chlorine capped around 0.2–0.4 ppm; CYA capped at 100 ppm.
  • Several states (e.g. Texas) prohibit cyanuric acid indoors entirely — consistent with this app's Indoor/Outdoor setting, which forces CYA off for indoor pools.
  • Most codes expect corrective action to be documented whenever a reading falls outside range — what was added, and the retest result — which is what the new "Corrective action taken" field on the Log tab is for.
  • Testing frequency varies the most: from once a day up to every 1–2 hours while the pool is open, depending on facility size/staffing and whether there's an automated chemical feeder.
  • Record retention requirements, where specified, commonly run 1–2 years, and inspectors generally expect records on-site or quickly producible.

Finding your own local requirements

There's no single federal rulebook — the CDC publishes a voluntary Model Aquatic Health Code that many states borrow from, but each state (and often each county) sets its own binding rules. If you operate a regulated pool/spa, search "[your state] health department swimming pool code" or contact your county environmental health office directly — they can tell you the exact testing frequency, parameters, ranges, and retention period that apply to your facility and permit type.
